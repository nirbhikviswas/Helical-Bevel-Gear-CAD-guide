Part 1 — Geometry

The Core Problem

Before touching SolidWorks or Excel, you need to understand why spiral bevel gears are geometrically different from every other gear type — and why a random helix simply will not work.

Why a Constant Helix Fails on a Cone

On a cylindrical spur gear, the radius is constant everywhere. A helix wraps around it like a spring — same pitch, same angle all the way around. Simple.

On a bevel gear, the radius grows continuously from the small end (toe) to the large end (heel). If you apply a constant-angle helix to a cone, it will be geometrically correct at exactly one cross-section and wrong everywhere else. The tooth will not converge toward the apex, and the gears will grind or jam rather than mesh.

💡
Key Insight

Every cross-section of a bevel gear tooth — taken perpendicular to the cone surface — must look like a correctly proportioned involute tooth for that particular radius. Because the radius changes from toe to heel, the spiral angle must also change continuously, following a specific mathematical formula. This is what the Excel integration computes.

What "Properly Meshing" Actually Requires

Three conditions must all be satisfied simultaneously for two bevel gears to mesh correctly:

  1. Same apex — both cones must share a single common apex point
  2. Conjugate profiles — at every cross-section, the tooth profiles must be involutes of the correct virtual gear
  3. Correct taper — tooth height must scale linearly with cone distance (shorter at toe, taller at heel)

This guide ensures all three conditions are met through proper calculation.

Apex & Cone Distance

The Apex

Imagine extending the slanted cone surface of each gear until the two cones meet at a single point. That point is the apex. It is the most fundamental concept in bevel gear geometry.

⚠️
The Apex Rule

Every line on every tooth surface of both gears must, when extended, pass through the common apex. Any geometry that violates this rule will cause the gears to jam, wear rapidly, or simply not turn.

Cone Distance — Your Position Variable

Instead of measuring position by radius (as on a spur gear), on a bevel gear you measure position along the cone surface from the apex. This is called cone distance, symbol t.

At any cone distance t, the pitch circle radius R and axial position Z are:

Geometry at cone distance t
R(t) = t × sin(Γ)    Z(t) = t × cos(Γ)
Γ = pitch cone angle = 45° for 1:1 ratio
At toe (t = 32.426): R = 32.426 × sin(45°) = 22.929 mm
At mean (t = 42.426): R = 42.426 × sin(45°) = 30.000 mm ← design reference
At heel (t = 52.426): R = 52.426 × sin(45°) = 37.071 mm

The Three Key Positions on the Tooth

Back Cone & Virtual Gear

Here is the concept that makes tooth profile design possible. At any cross-section perpendicular to the cone surface, the tooth profile looks exactly like a tooth belonging to a flat spur gear. The radius of that imaginary spur gear is the back cone radius — which equals the cone distance t at that section.

The number of teeth that imaginary spur gear would have is called the virtual tooth count Nv. Since the back cone radius is larger than the actual pitch circle radius, Nv is always larger than actual N:

Virtual tooth count
Nv = N / cos(Γ)
N = actual teeth = 20, Γ = pitch cone angle = 45°
Nv = 20 / cos(45°) = 20 / 0.7071 = 28.284

Base circle radius for involute:
rb = (m × Nv / 2) × cos(φ) = (3 × 28.284 / 2) × cos(20°) = 39.868 mm

This rb value is what you use when sketching the involute tooth profile in SolidWorks. The tooth flank is an involute of a 39.868 mm base circle — not of the 30 mm pitch circle.

Why the Spiral Angle Must Vary

The spiral angle β is measured between the tooth centreline and a straight radial line from the apex. At the mean section, β = 35° (our design choice). But it cannot be constant everywhere.

Physical reason: at the toe, the pitch circle circumference is small (radius 22.9 mm). At the heel it is large (37.1 mm). If the tooth had a constant spiral angle, it would rotate through very different arc lengths at the toe versus heel, violating the apex rule — the tooth centreline would not converge toward the apex.

Spiral angle at any cone distance t
β(t) = arctan( tan(β_mean) × t / Am )
β_mean = 35°, Am = 42.426 mm
At toe (t = 32.426): β = arctan(tan(35°) × 32.426/42.426) = arctan(0.5345) = 28.15°
At mean (t = 42.426): β = arctan(tan(35°) × 42.426/42.426) = arctan(0.7002) = 35.00° ✓
At heel (t = 52.426): β = arctan(tan(35°) × 52.426/42.426) = arctan(0.8659) = 40.87°
✦
Why this formula works

The ratio t/Am appears because arc length on the cone surface is proportional to cone distance t. At t = Am, the ratio is 1.0, so β = β_mean exactly. Below Am the tooth curves less; above Am it curves more — maintaining the apex convergence at every point.

Part 2 — Calculations

Pitch Cone Angle

The pitch cone angle Γ is the half-angle of each gear cone — the angle between the gear axis and the slanted cone surface. Everything else depends on it.

Pitch cone angle — valid for shaft angle = 90°
Γ₁ = arctan(N1 / N2)
N1 = pinion teeth, N2 = gear teeth. For other shaft angles: tan(Γ₁) = sin(Σ) / (N2/N1 + cos(Σ))
Γ₁ = arctan(20/20) = arctan(1.0) = 45.000°
Γ₂ = 90° − 45° = 45.000°

Both gears have 45° cone angles (unique to 1:1 ratio — both cones are identical)
📐
Why arctan(N1/N2)?

Two pitch cones rolling without slipping must have the same pitch-line velocity at their contact. This means their radii at the contact line must be proportional to N1:N2. Working through the geometry of two cones sharing an apex at 90°, you get tan(Γ₁) = sin(90°)/(N2/N1 + cos(90°)) = 1/(N2/N1) = N1/N2. At 90° the cos term vanishes, leaving the clean arctan formula.

Cone Distances

Mean pitch circle radius
R_mean = m × N / 2
Module m is defined as pitch diameter / teeth, so PD = m×N, and R = m×N/2
R_mean = 3 × 20 / 2 = 30.000 mm
Mean cone distance
Am = R_mean / sin(Γ₁)
From the right triangle: sin(Γ) = R_mean/Am → Am = R_mean/sin(Γ)
Am = 30.000 / sin(45°) = 30.000 / 0.70711 = 42.426 mm
Heel and Toe distances
Ao = Am + F/2     Ai = Am − F/2
F = face width = 20 mm (the tooth length along the cone surface)
Ao = 42.426 + 10.000 = 52.426 mm (heel, large end)
Ai = 42.426 − 10.000 = 32.426 mm (toe, small end)

Face Width Limits — Must Check Both

-- Face width F must satisfy BOTH of these -- Limit 1: F ≤ Ao/3 = 52.426/3 = 17.475 mm Limit 2: F ≤ 10×m = 10×3 = 30.000 mm Our F = 20 mm EXCEEDS Limit 1 (17.475 mm) For strict design use F = 17 mm. -- This document uses F=20 for the spiral tables (illustration purposes) -- -- For real manufacturing: F must be ≤ Ao/3 --

Tooth Height & The Taper Law

Bevel gear teeth are NOT the same height from toe to heel. They taper — shorter at the toe, taller at the heel — following a strict linear law. Getting this wrong is the most common modelling mistake.

Tooth depths at mean section (Gleason)
hw = 2 × m = 6.000 mm    h = 2.188×m + 0.05 = 6.614 mm
hw = working depth (addendum + mating addendum), h = whole depth (includes root clearance)
For 1:1 ratio: addendum a = hw/2 = m = 3.000 mm
Dedendum b = h − a = 6.614 − 3.000 = 3.614 mm
Root clearance = h − hw = 6.614 − 6.000 = 0.614 mm total
Taper law — tooth height at any cone distance x
h(x) = h_mean × (x / Am)
Linear scaling: tooth height is proportional to cone distance from apex
At toe (x = 32.426): h = 6.614 × (32.426/42.426) = 6.614 × 0.7643 = 5.053 mm
At mean (x = 42.426): h = 6.614 × (42.426/42.426) = 6.614 × 1.0000 = 6.614 mm ✓
At heel (x = 52.426): h = 6.614 × (52.426/42.426) = 6.614 × 1.2357 = 8.173 mm
✦
Why the taper is necessary

The tooth spacing (circular pitch) also scales linearly with radius — and therefore with cone distance. If tooth height were constant, the teeth would be too tall at the toe (relative to the spacing) causing interference, and too short at the heel causing a gap. The taper keeps the proportions correct everywhere. In SolidWorks, guide curves automatically enforce this taper during the sweep.

Involute Tooth Profile

The tooth flank shape is an involute curve, designed for the virtual tooth count Nv = 28.284, with base circle radius rb = 39.868 mm. Use these 10 points as a spline in the SolidWorks tooth profile sketch:

θ (rad)x = rb(cosθ + θ·sinθ)y = rb(sinθ − θ·cosθ)Note
📐
How to use in SolidWorks

In the tooth profile sketch: draw a Spline clicking each of the 10 (x,y) points in sequence. Then Mirror Entities about the Y-axis for the other flank. Add a root fillet arc (~0.9 mm radius) at the bottom and a flat tip line (~0.75 mm wide) at the top. The profile must be a fully closed shape.

Angular Integration — The Spiral Formula

The spiral curve is defined by how much it rotates angularly (angle φ around the cone axis) as cone distance t increases from Ai to Ao. This cannot be expressed in a simple closed form — it requires numerical integration, which is what the Excel table computes step by step.

Rate of angular rotation per unit cone distance
dφ/dt = tan(β(t)) / R(t) = tan(β(t)) / (t × sin(Γ))
β(t) = spiral angle at position t, R(t) = pitch circle radius at position t
Trapezoidal integration step (Excel column F, rows 3 onwards)
Δφᵢ = tan(avg_β) / avg_R × Δt
avg_β = average of β at current and previous row; avg_R = average of R; Δt = step in t
Excel formula for F3 (pinion, left hand):
=(TAN(RADIANS((E2+E3)/2))) / (((B2+B3)/2)*SIN(RADIANS(45))) * (B3-B2)

Excel formula for F3 (mating gear, right hand — only the sign changes):
=-(TAN(RADIANS((E2+E3)/2))) / (((B2+B3)/2)*SIN(RADIANS(45))) * (B3-B2)

The cumulative angle G3 = G2 + F3, and so on down to row 51. At step 50, φ = 0.4680 rad = 26.75° — this is the total angular sweep of the spiral from toe to heel.
